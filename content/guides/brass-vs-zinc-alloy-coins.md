# Zinc Alloy vs. Brass for Custom Challenge Coins: Density, Die-Casting Tonnage, and Tactile Cost-Efficiency

- **Density is the decision.** H65 brass (8.4–8.5 g/cm³) is about 27% denser than die-cast Zamak 3 zinc alloy (6.6–6.7 g/cm³), so the same coin weighs about 27% more in brass — the entire premium-heft gap in a single number.
- **Weight follows geometry.** A 40 mm × 3.0 mm (1.57" × 0.118") coin with deep relief weighs about 28 g in zinc and 36–38 g in brass; the 55–65 g (1.94–2.29 oz) heft buyers expect from a standard-format coin is a brass-at-44-mm proposition — or a thicker zinc blank.
- **Relief depth picks the process.** Stamped brass holds crisp detail to 0.45 mm (0.018") of single-side relief; die-cast zinc carries deep multi-step 3D relief of 0.80–1.80 mm (0.031"–0.071") plus open cutouts.
- **Tooling pays once and runs long.** A steel die is rated for 50,000–100,000 shots, sampling takes 3–7 working days, bulk production 12–18 working days, and the minimum order is 300 pieces.

Pick up two challenge coins of the same size — same diameter, same thickness, same gold finish. One settles into the palm with quiet authority; the other feels like a token from an arcade. Nothing about the artwork explains the difference. The gap is inside the metal: brass is roughly a quarter denser than die-cast zinc, and that single physical fact decides weight, hand feel, relief depth, and cost before a single sketch is drawn.

## Weight Is a Density Decision, Not a Size Decision

A challenge coin is, to a first approximation, a disc. Its weight is face area × thickness × density, and only the last term changes when you switch alloys. Zamak 3 zinc alloy runs 6.6–6.7 g/cm³; H65 brass runs 8.4–8.5 g/cm³. Divide one by the other and the result is 1.27: at identical geometry, a brass coin is about 27% heavier than its zinc twin. No plating, no packaging, no presentation box changes that ratio — it is fixed by the periodic table.

That ratio is the whole commercial story. Buyers who call a coin cheap-feeling are almost always describing a light coin, and buyers who call one premium-feeling are describing a heavy one. The table below shows what the two alloys actually weigh across the formats buyers order most, computed with our lab's volume model at a flat edge:

| Coin format | Relief | Zinc (Zamak 3) | Brass (H65) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 mm × 3.0 mm (1.57" × 0.118") | Flat / enamel-filled | 24.2 g (0.85 oz) | 30.8 g (1.09 oz) |
| 40 mm × 3.0 mm (1.57" × 0.118") | Deep 3D | 28.6 g (1.01 oz) | 36.3 g (1.28 oz) |
| 44 mm × 3.5 mm (1.75" × 0.138") | Semi-relief | 37.3 g (1.32 oz) | 47.3 g (1.67 oz) |
| 44 mm × 4.5 mm (1.75" × 0.177") | Semi-relief | 47.5 g (1.68 oz) | 60.2 g (2.12 oz) |
| 50 mm × 3.5 mm (2.0" × 0.138") | Semi-relief | 48.1 g (1.70 oz) | 61.1 g (2.16 oz) |

Two things stand out. First, relief matters: moving from a flat enamel-filled face to deep 3D relief adds roughly 4–5 g at 40 mm, because the raised metal is real metal. Second, the 55–65 g (1.94–2.29 oz) heft band that buyers associate with a serious standard-format coin is not reachable at 44 mm × 3.5 mm in either alloy — it takes a 44 mm brass blank at about 4.5 mm thick (60.2 g), or a step up to 50 mm. In zinc, the same feel demands a thicker blank still: brass reaches 55 g at roughly 4.3 mm of thickness, zinc needs roughly 5.4 mm. That is the density gap expressing itself as a thickness decision, and it is why making a coin feel heavier usually means choosing brass rather than going bigger.

Diameter works the same way. Going from 40 mm to 45 mm adds about 27% more face area, so weight rises by the same proportion at fixed thickness — a cheap way to add presence in zinc, and an expensive way to add it in brass, where every extra gram is the denser metal. ## Relief Depth Picks the Process

Weight decides how a coin feels. Relief decides how it is made — and the two alloys do not share a process at all.

Stamped brass is cold-formed: a blank of H65 sheet is struck under a hydraulic coining press, and the metal flows into the die's shallow cavities. The practical ceiling is 0.45 mm (0.018") of single-side relief. That sounds limiting, and it is — but within that envelope, stamping produces the crispest lines and sharpest lettering of any coin process, because the metal is displaced rather than poured. The constraint to respect is balance: a deep relief on the face with a flat back leaves uneven residual stress, and the blank can cup or warp as it springs back. Our tooling engineers counter this with counter-relief — a matching recess or secondary motif on the reverse that balances the metal flow.

Die-cast zinc works the other way. Molten Zamak 3 at 410–430 °C (770–806 °F) is injected into a steel die, and liquid metal fills cavities no press could ever reach: deep multi-step 3D relief of 0.80–1.80 mm (0.031"–0.071"), open cutouts, sculptural forms, and complex outlines in a single shot. The limits are different ones: walls must stay at or above 1.0 mm (0.039") so the melt fills completely, vertical faces need 1.5°–3.0° of draft so the casting releases, and wall thickness must stay reasonably uniform or the cooling metal shrinks into sink marks that show through plating.

| | Die-cast zinc (Zamak 3) | Stamped brass (H65) |
|---|---|---|
| Density | 6.6–6.7 g/cm³ | 8.4–8.5 g/cm³ |
| Forming | Molten zinc, 410–430 °C (770–806 °F), hot-chamber die-casting | Cold stamping under a hydraulic coining press |
| Relief ceiling | 0.80–1.80 mm (0.031"–0.071") deep 3D, multi-step | 0.45 mm (0.018") single-side |
| Cutouts and sculptural forms | Yes — open cutouts, 3D shapes | No — flat blank, shallow relief only |
| Best for | Deep sculptural relief, complex outlines | Crisp shallow detail, sharp lettering, premium heft |
| Watch out | Keep walls uniform to avoid sink marks; 1.5°–3.0° draft | One-sided relief needs counter-relief to prevent warping |

Detail limits apply to both processes and are set by the enamel, not the alloy: raised metal lines must be at least 0.25 mm (0.010") for soft enamel and 0.35 mm (0.014") for hard enamel, or plating buildup bridges across them and the color bleeds; lettering should stay at or above 5 pt in a bold sans-serif face. If your artwork has fine text wrapped around a deep-relief portrait, that combination alone usually settles the process question — the portrait wants die-casting, and the text wants a flat field with generous line weights. ## Tooling, Lead Time, and the Real Cost Picture

The cost question buyers actually ask is rarely which alloy is cheaper in the abstract — it is what the whole order costs, and how long until it ships. The honest answer starts with tooling. A hardened steel die is rated for 50,000–100,000 shots, which means the tooling cost is a one-time investment spread across the first order and every reorder after it; repeat orders reuse the same die and skip the tooling charge entirely. That long die life is what makes a 300-piece minimum order viable for a fully custom coin.

From artwork approval, the clock runs like this: 2D/3D artwork and rendering in 2–4 hours, a physical sample in 3–7 working days, and bulk production in 12–18 working days. The alloy choice moves the per-piece economics inside that frame rather than the schedule: brass costs more metal per coin because each coin carries about 27% more mass, while die-cast zinc absorbs complex three-dimensional artwork into a single casting step that stamping cannot reproduce at any price. The right comparison is never zinc-versus-brass in the abstract — it is your artwork, at your quantity, against the relief it needs.

Durability is the last check, and it is the same for both alloys: finished coins are validated against a 48–72 hour neutral salt spray test, and outgoing inspection follows AQL 1.5 Major / 4.0 Minor sampling under a D65 daylight booth. A coin that passes those checks will not blacken in a drawer or flake at the rim — whichever alloy you chose for its weight.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Which metal feels heavier at the same size?**
A: Brass, by about 27%. H65 brass (8.4–8.5 g/cm³) is roughly a quarter denser than Zamak 3 zinc alloy (6.6–6.7 g/cm³), so any coin in brass weighs about 27% more than the identical coin in zinc. If heft is the priority and the artwork is shallow, brass is the direct answer; if the artwork needs deep relief, zinc with a thicker blank or a larger diameter is the way to reach the same feel.

**Q: Can I get deep 3D relief in brass?**
A: Not by stamping. Cold stamping caps out at about 0.45 mm (0.018") of single-side relief before the metal stops flowing cleanly and the blank risks warping. Deep multi-step 3D relief of 0.80–1.80 mm (0.031"–0.071"), open cutouts, and sculptural forms need die-cast zinc. If your design mixes a deep-relief portrait with fine text, the practical solution is a die-cast zinc coin with the text on a flat field.

**Q: What are the lead time and minimum order?**
A: The minimum order is 300 pieces. Artwork and 3D rendering take 2–4 hours, a physical sample takes 3–7 working days, and bulk production takes 12–18 working days. The steel die is rated for 50,000–100,000 shots, so reorders reuse the same tooling with no new die charge.
