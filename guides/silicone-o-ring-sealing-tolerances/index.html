Food-Grade Silicone O-Ring Tolerances and Friction Coefficients in Bottle Neck Sealing

2026-11-24 · 11 min read · GoldValley Engineering Lab

  • The bore is a moving target. The standard bottleneck measures 18.5 mm (0.73") ± 0.5 mm (0.020") across bottle makers — a full 1.0 mm (0.039") band — so no fixed-diameter plug can seal it. Only a stepped ring stack spanning 17.0–20.0 mm (0.67"–0.79") covers the whole band.
  • Interference is the seal. Top-ring interference runs 0.5–2.0 mm (0.020"–0.079") across the bore band, or 2.6–11.1% of bore diameter. Below 0.3 mm (0.012") of interference the lab flags a loose fit — and a loose fit is a leak.
  • Hardness Shore A 45–55 is the compromise. Soft enough to compress up to 2.0 mm at the narrowest bore without excessive push-in force, firm enough to hold its shape and rebound after hundreds of insertions — food-grade LSR/VMQ, post-cured at 200 °C (392 °F) for 2–4 hours.
  • Pull force 20–35 N (4.5–7.9 lbf) is the comfort window. And the seal is nearly half the anti-tilt system: for the lab's default stag head, the silicone friction bonus supplies 45% of the restoring moment.

The failure never announces itself on day one. A metal stopper slides in, feels snug, and works — until a week later the wine tastes flat, or the bottle is knocked in the door rack and the stopper leans, then tips. The cause is rarely the metal head: it is the millimetre of air between a silicone ring and a bottle neck that was never the size the drawing assumed. Sealing a wine bottle is not making a plug that fits; it is making a seal that fits a bore that changes from one glass factory to the next.

The Bore Is a Moving Target: 18.5 mm ± 0.5 mm

The standard for a still-wine bottleneck is EN 12726 / CETIE GME 50.01: a bore of 18.5 mm (0.73") ± 0.5 mm (0.020"), maximum ovality 0.5 mm (0.020"), inner wall controlled over the top 45 mm (1.77"). That band is the whole story: one bottle's bore is 18.0 mm (0.709"), the next is 19.0 mm (0.748") — both in spec, both to seal with the same stopper.

A single fixed-diameter plug cannot do that. Machined to the nominal 18.5 mm, it leaves a 0.5 mm diametral gap in a 19.0 mm bore — 0.25 mm (0.010") of radial clearance all the way around the seal, an open path for air — and carries 0.5 mm of interference in an 18.0 mm bore, where a rigid plug does not compress. To hold at all it needs at least 0.3 mm (0.012") of interference at the widest bore — the lab's loose-fit threshold — so an outside diameter of 19.3 mm (0.760") or more, hence 1.3 mm (0.051") at the narrowest. One ring, one diameter, one failure or the other. And the bore is not always round: with 0.5 mm of ovality allowed, it can be 18.5 mm in one direction and 19.0 mm in the other, so a rigid seal leaks on the wide axis while a soft one takes the load on the narrow axis. Only an elastomer can follow both at once — which is why every working metal stopper seals with silicone, not with metal.

The Stepped Stack: Three to Five Rings, One Job Each

The working answer is a stepped stack of three to five tapered silicone rings, their outside diameter growing from about 17.0–17.5 mm (0.67"–0.69") at the bottom ring to about 19.5–20.0 mm (0.77"–0.79") at the top. The rings do different jobs: the lower rings never seal — at 17.0–17.5 mm they clear even the narrowest 18.0 mm bore by 0.5–2.0 mm (0.020"–0.079"), so they guide and centre the stem and take no compression — while the upper rings compress against the bore wall and lock with elastic friction. The stem finds centre first, then resistance builds smoothly as the upper rings engage, instead of jamming on the first millimetre.

Bore diameter Top-ring interference Interference as % of bore Bottom-ring clearance
18.0 mm (0.709") 1.5–2.0 mm (0.059"–0.079") 8.3–11.1% 0.5–1.0 mm (0.020"–0.039")
18.5 mm (0.728") 1.0–1.5 mm (0.039"–0.059") 5.4–8.1% 1.0–1.5 mm (0.039"–0.059")
19.0 mm (0.748") 0.5–1.0 mm (0.020"–0.039") 2.6–5.3% 1.5–2.0 mm (0.059"–0.079")

The worst case is the one that matters: a top ring at the small end of its range (19.5 mm) in the widest in-spec bore (19.0 mm) still carries 0.5 mm of interference — above the lab's 0.3 mm loose-fit line, with margin. The best case — a 20.0 mm top ring in an 18.0 mm bore — is 2.0 mm, and the ring absorbs it because it is silicone at the right hardness. Between those extremes the stack seals every in-spec bottle.

Spreading the load also keeps the seal alive: a single ring compressed 2.0 mm takes a permanent set faster than three rings sharing it, and a set ring no longer springs back. Across three to five rings each stays inside its elastic range, so the hundredth insertion seals like the first. The handle and stem are joined with an M4–M6 thread or an anti-rotation interference pin, so twisting the handle to pull the stopper never works the rings loose.

Hardness Is the Compromise: Shore A 45–55

The specification is Shore A 45–55 food-grade silicone, a narrow range because each side of it fails differently. Go softer than 45 and the rings compress beautifully on day one and then stop coming back — a soft ring takes a compression set, holding its squeezed shape after the stopper is pulled, so the next insertion meets a ring already smaller than its drawing size and the interference that was the seal quietly disappears. Go harder than 55 and the rings cannot compress the full 2.0 mm at the narrow end of the bore band without excessive push-in force, and they conform worse to the 0.5 mm of ovality the standard allows, so the seal leaks on the wide axis of an oval bore. Harder is not tighter — the seal comes from interference plus friction, and 45–55 is where a ring compresses far enough, springs back reliably, and still grips.

The material is liquid silicone rubber (LSR) or vinyl methyl silicone (VMQ), food-contact grade — never ordinary rubber or soft PVC, which can leach plasticizers into the wine. After moulding, the rings are post-cured at 200 °C (392 °F) for 2–4 hours to drive off volatile residues, so the seal adds no odour and no flavour. Platinum-cured is preferred over peroxide-cured: fewer volatiles, less risk of an off-note in an acidic wine.

Compliance is a specification, not a slogan. Silicone rings for food contact must meet GB 4806.16-2025 — the dedicated national standard for food-contact silicone rubber, in force since 2026-09-02, replacing the silicone-rubber part of GB 4806.11-2016 — and FDA 21 CFR 177.2600 for repeated-use rubber articles. For Germany the practical bar is BfR Recommendation XV: volatiles at or below 0.5% after 200 °C for 4 hours, plus a DIN 10955 sensory assessment. Under (EC) No 1935/2004 Article 16 the supplier must provide a Declaration of Compliance; a regulation number on its own, without a DoC, is not compliance in the EU. Wine is acidic (pH 3.0–3.8), so the decorative head never touches it: everything below the rim is a food-grade stainless steel core — SUS304 as standard, SUS316L for high-acid wines or long soaks — under GB 4806.9-2023, and the silicone rings do all of the sealing.

Friction Does the Sealing: Pull Force and the Three Lab Bases

A seal that cannot be opened is a defect, not a feature. The comfortable pull force for a finished metal stopper is 20–35 N (4.5–7.9 lbf): firm enough to trust that the bottle will not leak on its side, light enough to open one-handed. Below 20 N the stopper feels loose; above 35 N it feels stuck. The ring stack holds a stopper inside that window across the whole bore band — the same interference that seals is the friction that resists the pull.

The lab models this as three base configurations, and running them across the bore band shows how much margin each one carries:

Base configuration Max insert diameter Interference at 18.0 / 18.5 / 19.0 mm bore Friction torque bonus Seal share of restoring moment*
Zamak 3 tapered stem + 3-rib silicone (26.5 g) 20.5 mm (0.807") 2.5 / 2.0 / 1.5 mm 9.5 N·mm 45%
Heavy SUS304 CNC stem + silicone (38.0 g) 20.8 mm (0.819") 2.8 / 2.3 / 1.8 mm 13.5 N·mm 52%
Solid brass core + natural cork (22.0 g) 19.8 mm (0.780") 1.8 / 1.3 / 0.8 mm 6.5 N·mm 37%

* Seal share computed for the lab's default stag head (104.1 g, composite CG +27.6 mm above the rim) at a 15° tilt; restoring totals are 20.9 / 26.0 / 17.5 N·mm.

Two things stand out. First, all three bases stay inside the lab's optimal fit zone across the standard bore band — none drops below the 0.3 mm loose-fit line. The cork base runs out of margin first: it crosses that line just past a 19.5 mm bore, which is why the lab rates cork as lower elastic retention than ribbed silicone — cork belongs on spirits and T-stoppers, not on a wine stopper that must seal the full band. Second, the heavy stainless stem is not just a counterweight: its 13.5 N·mm friction bonus is the largest of the three, so the seal supplies 52% of the restoring moment that keeps the assembly upright — more than gravity does.

That second point is the one buyers miss: the seal is also the anti-tilt system. For the default stag head, the overturning moment at a 15° tilt is 9.2 N·mm; the restoring moment is 20.9 N·mm with the silicone stem, a safety factor of 2.29× — and 9.5 N·mm of that is the silicone ribs gripping the bore wall, not the weight of the assembly. Swap to the cork base and it falls to 17.5 N·mm. A leak and a tip are the same failure wearing two costumes: once the rib on one side is permanently compressed, air creeps past the seal and the stopper leans, and each makes the other worse.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will one stopper really fit every standard wine bottle?

Yes, within the standard band: a stepped stack spanning 17.0–20.0 mm holds 0.5–2.0 mm of top-ring interference on every bore from 18.0 mm to 19.0 mm, and the lab's standard base is rated for necks from 17.5 mm to 19.5 mm. Outside that — flared champagne necks past 19.2 mm, or narrow half-bottle and ice-wine necks below 17.5 mm — the stack runs out of margin, so name the bottle in the RFQ.

Why not use a harder silicone ring for a tighter seal?

Harder is not tighter. Above Shore A 55 the ring cannot compress the full 2.0 mm at the narrow end of the bore band without excessive push-in force, and it conforms worse to the 0.5 mm of ovality the standard allows, so it leaks on the wide axis of an oval bore. Below 45 the ring compresses easily but takes a permanent set and stops springing back. The seal comes from interference plus friction, and Shore A 45–55 is where a ring compresses far enough, rebounds reliably, and still grips.

Is the silicone safe next to wine?

Yes, when it is the right silicone: food-grade LSR or VMQ — never ordinary rubber or soft PVC — post-cured at 200 °C for 2–4 hours, meeting GB 4806.16-2025 and FDA 21 CFR 177.2600, with a Declaration of Compliance for the EU market. The metal head never touches the wine: everything below the rim is a SUS304 stainless core carrying the silicone rings, which do all of the sealing.
